memory is not a recording device. it's a {fold: reconstruction process | every time you recall something, you rebuild it from fragments. this means memories are susceptible to change each time you access them — a fact with profound implications for how we learn.}

## types of memory

- **working memory** — holds roughly 7 items for seconds to minutes
- **episodic memory** — autobiographical events with context and emotion
- **semantic memory** — facts and concepts stripped of their learning context
- **procedural memory** — how to do things; hard to articulate but durable

## the forgetting curve

without review, we forget exponentially fast.

::: branch ebbinghaus and the forgetting curve
Hermann Ebbinghaus charted his own memory decay in 1885 by memorizing and testing nonsense syllables.

his findings: after 1 hour, ~56% forgotten. after 1 day, ~66% forgotten. after 1 week, ~75% forgotten.

but each review resets the curve and extends the next retention window. this is the foundation of spaced repetition.
:::

## how to improve retention

memory improves with {fold: spaced repetition | reviewing information at increasing intervals. the key insight is that retrieving information strengthens the memory trace more than re-reading it. active recall beats passive review.}

other evidence-backed techniques:

- **interleaving** — mixing topics during study sessions
- **elaborative interrogation** — asking *why* something is true, not just *what*
- **concrete examples** — abstract concepts stick better when grounded

## connection to learning

you cannot separate memory from [[learning]]. encoding, consolidation, and retrieval are all part of the same cycle. improving one improves the other.
